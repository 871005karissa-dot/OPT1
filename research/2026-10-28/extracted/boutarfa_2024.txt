A B S T R A C T
Inspired by a real-work case, this paper introduces a profit maximization model for dynamic lot sizing
considering substitution and multiple usage of returns for refurbishing at different levels of quality or for
disassembly to extract key parts that will be used in the manufacturing process. This model allows studying
the interactions between different types of returns and decisions. To analyze the impact of uncertainty on
these interactions, we develop a robust two-stage stochastic program with uncertainty on demands and returns.
The resulting problems are mixed-integer linear programs that we solve using an efficient relax-and-fix and
fix-and-optimize heuristic. Extensive numerical experiments are conducted to study the different trade-offs
when integrating multi-usage of returns with substitution and accordingly derive managerial insights. The
experiments have revealed, for example, that: (i) the profit margin of the refurbished items is the main
determinant of the total profit and when such margin in high, the total profit becomes more sensitive to
different cost variations; (ii) collection efficiency becomes very sensitive to collection cost and much less
sensitive to refurbishing cost especially with large profit margins; (iii) when demand and returns are uncertain
parameters, substitution becomes the best option as uncertainty and prices increase; (iv) when the gap
between prices of different quality levels is high, lost sales occur mainly on the lowest quality product and
downgrading decreases together with substitution; and (v) Sharing the production line between refurbishing
and manufacturing for low-quality products is highly motivated by small upgrading costs and their substitution
level increases with increasing upgrading costs.
1. Introduction
In 2022, there was 62 billion kg of e-waste (electronic waste) world-
wide. This quantity can cover the area of an entire city. 1 These wastes
can be considerably reduced if circular economy principles are imple-
mented by companies, moving from the traditional linear systems (take,
make, dispose) to a closed loop system in which products are reused,
shared, repaired, refurbished, remanufactured and recycled (Invernizzi
et al. , 2020 ). Companies are shifting to circular systems because of
pressures from the society and governments in addition to the scarcity
of resources. Hence companies have been urged to accelerate their
search for more sustainable solutions ( Rogers et al. , 1999; Krajewski
et al. , 2016 ). This is the case for a large consumer electronics with
which the authors of this paper have collaborated. The company was
looking for solutions to develop its closed-loop supply chain with the
most efficient reverse logistics processes. The authors collaborated with

demand for refurbished products can be satisfied using new items. The
downward substitution at the finished goods level occurs in practice
when there are different market segments for new and refurbished
products. In this situation a customer’s demand for some refurbished
product arrives when such product is out of stock. The planner may
choose to use the inventory of some new product to fulfill the demand
and charge the price of the refurbished product. This can avoid shortage
and setup cost of directly producing the refurbished product. It can also
lead to opportunities to create greater flexibility in their production and
inventory planning.
The objective is to maximize profit while fully satisfying the demand
for new products and attempting to meet the demand for used (refur-
bished) products. The profit is calculated as the difference between the
sales revenue and the total costs, which include disassembly, produc-
tion and inventory holding costs among others. The demand for new
products must be fully satisfied while shortages are allowed for used
products. Over a discrete time horizon with a dynamic demand, the
planner must decide for each time period the quantity of items that
can be collected and stocked to enter the process, the quantity to be
disassembled and whose key component is used for manufacturing new
products, the quantity of the new key part to be purchased for the
manufacturing of new products, the quantity of used items that will
be refurbished, and the quantity of new items that will be used for
substitution. From the disassembly process, we focus on the extraction
of a single key part, resulting in a 1:1:1 relationship, i.e. one unit
of a disassembled returned item generates one key part, which will
be used in the manufacturing of one unit of the finished new item.
This study explicitly distinguishes between refurbishing (used second
hand finished products) and remanufacturing (spare parts) following
the framework and terminology proposed by Thierry et al. (1995).
The latter proposed that return options can be divided into repair, re-
furbishing, remanufacturing, cannibalization, and recycling. Using old
parts in the assembly of new products is considered as cannibalization
and remanufacturing, according to the definitions presented by Thierry
et al. (1995). More details on the description and formulation of the
problem are presented in Section 3.1.
This study can be divided into three main parts: A first part where
a single type of products is sold either as a used refurbished item or as
a new item. In the second part, a stochastic model with uncertainty on
the collected items and demand for used items is proposed. A two-stage
stochastic model is used in this case and several managerial insights are
provided. In this same part, a risk averse stochastic programming model
is proposed. Finally, we discuss different trade-offs that take place when
products of different values and quality grading are considered in the
process. Such a system results in more complex flows between different
processes in the system. The paper also presents an efficient heuristic to
solve the corresponding planning problems and analyzes the economic
and ecological advantages of substitution and integration of decisions
between used and new products.
The main contributions of the paper can be summarized as follows:
• It provides a literature review specific to lot sizing with refurbish-
ing and substitution, which has not been done before, to the best
of our knowledge.
• It introduces a profit maximization integrated model for dynamic
lot sizing considering substitution and multiple usage of returns
for refurbishing at different levels of quality (through upgrading
or downgrading) or for disassembly to extract key parts that will
be used in the manufacturing process. The model was inspired by
a real case from industry.
• It proposes a stochastic optimization model with uncertainty on
demand for used items and on the returned items.
• It proposes an efficient relax-and-fix and fix-and-optimize heuris-
tic to solve large instances of the integrated model.
• It presents extensive numerical analysis and derives managerial
insights that show the importance of substitution, multi-usage
of returns, and consideration of uncertainty and multi-grades of
products.
Despite the increase in research on reverse logistics, to the best
of our knowledge, there are no publications on the integrated deci-
sions of the production planning of new products, refurbished products
(with lower value than the new ones), and parts (as good as new
parts) obtained from disassembly of returned products, alongside down-
ward substitution (demand for refurbished products satisfied using new
products). However, as indicated above, companies are interested in us-
ing existing assembly lines for disassembly and refurbishing purposes,
justifying the integration of such decisions. Furthermore, downward
substitution is a significant tool used in the semiconductor industry and
computer retail (e.g. Ervolina et al., 2006; Gallego et al., 2006).
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2
presents a review of recent publications relevant to our work. The prob-
lem is described in detail and formulated in Section 3. Section 4 details
the heuristic solution approach. Section 5 presents the numerical exper-
iments used to analyze the performance of the heuristic approach. This
section also presents several managerial insights. Finally, conclusions
are presented in Section 6.
2. Literature review
This section summarizes the publications relevant to our study. We
start by presenting general papers related to reverse logistics and hybrid
systems with substitution. Then, we present some production planning
problems with returns, and we focus on studies that address the lot
sizing problem with returns with substitution. The main aspects of these
studies are summarized in Table 1.
2.1. Reverse logistics and hybrid production systems with substitution
Recent literature surveys on reverse logistics and closed loop supply
chains have shown that thousands of scientific articles have been pub-
lished on these topics during the last 30 years. Some of these surveys
include those of Guide and Van Wassenhove (2009), Govindan et al.
(2015), Govindan and Soleimani (2017), and Kazemi et al. (2019).
For example, Rizova et al. (2020) identified hundreds of relevant
publications in recent years. This literature review focuses on studies
related to our research, namely production planning decisions related
to returns rather than the design of closed loop supply chains and
planning in reverse logistics in general.
The literature on the processing options for returned products
presents different streams and terminologies. For example, although
there is a difference between the terms ‘‘refurbishing’’ (or ‘‘refurbish-
ment’’) and ‘‘remanufacturing’’, several authors have been using them
interchangeably (e.g. Vorasayan and Ryan, 2006). It can be deduced
that there are two main streams. The first follows the classification
and terminology proposed by Thierry et al. (1995), which has been
considered by several authors (e.g., Gungor and Gupta, 1999; King
et al., 2006; Zikopoulos and Tagaras, 2007; Konstantaras et al., 2010;
Rachih et al., 2019). The article by Zacharaki et al. (2020), is related
to a recent project funded by the European Commission. Thierry et al.
(1995) proposed that product recovery can take the following forms:
repair, refurbishing, remanufacturing, cannibalization, and recycling.
In the second stream, researchers follow a simple categorization ini-
tially proposed by Fleischmann et al. (1997), which classifies recovery
into recycling (material recovery) and remanufacturing (value-added
recovery). See also Guide and Van Wassenhove (2009).
We followed the terminology used by Thierry et al. (1995). In par-
ticular, distinction is clearly made between refurbishing where critical
modules are inspected and upgraded to a specified quality level, and
Remanufacturing where modules and parts are inspected and upgraded
to as good as new quality.
Companies may use their new product manufacturing plants to
disassemble, remanufacture, or refurbish used products. These systems
are referred to as hybrid systems. They can take different forms. For

semble, refurbish, or remanufacture returned products. In this case, the
changeover times and costs should be considered. Other systems may

Managing production in hybrid systems is more challenging than in
classical assembly lines of new products. Difficulties can arise from the
complexity of the design decisions (e.g., Gungor and Gupta, 1998) and
planning decisions on which items to refurbish or remanufacture and
which parts to disassemble or recycle (Teunter et al., 2006; Cunha and
Melo, 2016). Another source of complexity is the uncertainty in both
the demand and returns of the used products (Fang et al., 2017; Naeem
et al., 2013; Assid et al., 2019; Macedo et al., 2016; Kilic and Tunc,
2019), the uncertainly in refurbishing duration (Slama et al., 2022),
and the uncertainty on the quality of returned products (Quezada et al.,
2020). Furthermore, such systems become harder to model and solve
when considering different types of products with different levels of
quality and when substitution is allowed between products.
Substitution, particularly downward substitution, has been used
as a strategy in the automotive industry (Ahiska and Kurtul, 2014).
Earlier studies on substitution that did not consider returns or reverse
logistics include Mcgillivray and Silver (1978), Pasternack and Drezner
(1991) and Benkherouf et al. (2017). In dynamic demand models,
contributions include those of Balakrishnan and Geunes (2000), Geunes
(2003), Yaman (2009) and Jing and Mu (2020), in addition to the
book by Lang and Domschke (2010). Shin et al. (2015) present a
recent survey and literature classification. Substitution can occur both
at the early stages of the production process (choice between used
and new parts) and at the finished goods level. Substitution at the
early stages raises several legal and technical issues. If it is possible to
choose between a new purchased part and a used part which has been
inspected and is considered as good as new (this is case in our paper),
we would not consider this as substitution as it will not be possible to
distinguish between finished goods containing either type of parts. If
the parts are just refurbished (have undergone less rigorous inspections
compared to remanufactured part), then the finished product cannot
be considered as new. This is different from our model. As indicated in
the Introduction section of the paper, downward substitution at the fin-
ished goods level occurs when there are different market segments for
new and refurbished products. Industrial applications where segmented
market for manufactured new products and refurbished products occurs
include photocopiers, tires and personal computers (Piñeyro and Viera,
2010; Li et al., 2006) and memory chips (Leachman and Glassey,
1987) It should be noted that despite the large number of publications
on production planning for remanufacturing and hybrid systems, few
publications consider substitution.
2.2. Dynamic lot sizing with product returns
The problem under consideration in this study is directly related
to the lot sizing problem with product returns (Teunter, 2004) and
more particularly to the dynamic version of the problem (Teunter
et al., 2006). The earliest known publication on the dynamic lot sizing
problem is the seminal paper by Wagner and Whitin (1958). They
proposed dynamic programming algorithms for the single item lot
sizing problem with time varying demand and without capacity re-
strictions. Several extensions of this problem have been reported in
the literature. Researchers have considered problems such as limited
production capacity, parameter uncertainty, non-linear costs, and lost
sales. Recent comprehensive surveys have been conducted on lot sizing
problems and their extensions, complexity, and solution approaches
(Jans and Degraeve, 2008; Buschkühl et al., 2010; Brahimi et al.,
2017; Gicquel et al., 2008; Quadt and Kuhn, 2008; Suwondo and
Yuliando, 2012). Most of the extensions of the uncapacitated single
item lot sizing problem are NP-hard, although polynomial algorithms
have been developed for certain special cases. For example, the single
item capacitated dynamic lot sizing problem is NP-hard in general,
but it becomes polynomial when the capacity is constant over all time
periods. The consideration of lot sizing with returns is one of the most
important research directions recommended in the survey by Brahimi
et al. (2017). Here, a review of publications in this area is given with
focus on the most relevant to current paper.
When considering problems with returns, the corresponding publi-
cations can be broadly classified into discrete and continuous models.

The discrete models that address the dynamic lot sizing problem with
product returns were first developed by Richter and Sombrutzki (2000),
Richter and Weber (2001), and Teunter et al. (2006). Inventory control
models with returns were discussed by Inderfurth (2004), Jaber and
El Saadany (2009), Mitra (2016), Wang et al. (2017), and Fang et al.
(2017).
Because of the complexity of lot sizing decisions in remanufac-
turing/refurbishing and hybrid systems, most proposed solution ap-
proaches in the literature are heuristics. An exception is the polyno-
mial time dynamic programming algorithm proposed by Teunter et al.
(2006), for a problem with separate setups and time invariant costs,
and Arulselvan and van den Heuvel (2022) for a problem with certain
realistic assumptions on the cost structure. Retel Helmrich et al. (2014)
discussed the complexity of dynamic lot sizing problems with returns
and showed that all lot sizing problems with returns are NP-hard,
except for the problem solved by Teunter et al. (2006). Heuristics used
to solve NP-hard versions of this problem include extensions of the
classical lot sizing heuristics such as Silver and Meal (1973) and Schulz
(2011); a dynamic programming based heuristic proposed by Fazle
Baki et al. (2014); metaheuristics such as the variable neighborhood
search proposed by Sifaleras et al. (2015), the tabu search heuristic
developed by Piñeyro and Viera (2009); a memetic algorithm and a
hybrid algorithm suggested by Zouadi et al. (2015). For the multi-item
problem, Sahling (2013) developed a column generation approach with
particular capacity constraints, whereas (Sifaleras and Konstantaras,
2017) solved another variant of the multi-item lot sizing problem with
returns using a variable neighborhood descent heuristic.
We will now discuss previous research work on lot sizing with
returns and substitution. Though our focus will be on dynamic lot sizing
models, we will present contributions on inventory control models
considering returns and substitution. The closest references to our study
are summarized in Table 1. The table lists the main attributes of each
paper. References are classified as follows:
• Demand (dem): stochastic (sto) or deterministic (det).
• The planning horizon (horizon): finite (fin) or infinite (inf), dis-
crete (disc), continuous (cont) or single period.
• With returns inventory (ret inv): Yes/No.
• Component inventory (Comp. inv.): Yes/No.
• Processing Capacities (proc capa): ‘‘No’’ if the capacity is infinite,
‘‘Yes’’ if any of the processes is capacitated, ‘‘Shared’’ if several
processes share the same resource(s).
• Setups: indicate cases with ‘‘no setup’’ costs or times and cases
with ‘‘separate’’ or ‘‘joint’’ setups;
• Activity type: manufacturing (mf), remanufacturing (rmf), refur-
bishing (rfb), cannibalization (cann), and new component replen-
ishment (comp).
• Usage of Returns indicates whether returns are used in both man-
ufacturing and remanufacturing/refurbishing processes (Multi) or
not (Mono).
• Substitution: whether it is downward, upward, or bidirectional
substitution.
• Objectives: indicates number and type of objectives.
• Solution approaches: indicates which solution is used to solve the
problem.
Table 1 shows that only six (out of 12 references) have proposed dy-
namic lot sizing models with returns and substitution. Also, more than
half of the models have stochastic demands (8 out of 12 references).
All these references consider continuous time or single period time
horizons except for the study by Liu et al. (2019). The latter assume
that both the amount of returns and demand for remanufactured items
are stochastic. They proposed an ant colony algorithm with a random
sampling method to solve the problem. Despite the importance and
challenges of storing returns, some studies have ignored these aspects.
For example, Bayındır et al. (2007) assumed that there is a continuous
flow of returns with a fixed cost that can be included directly in the
remanufacturing cost.
To the best of our knowledge, only two papers consider cannibal-
ization, i.e. the use of extracted parts from a returned items through
disassembly: Fang et al. (2017) and Marshall and Archibald (2015).
Compared to our work, the models in these two references assume
infinite and continuous time horizons. In the current work, we study
a dynamic demand finite time horizon model which is more suitable
for production planning with high demand variability. While Inderfurth
(2004), Li et al. (2006), and Piñeyro and Viera (2010) assumed infinite
processing capacity, all the other references in Table 1 assume finite
capacity processes. In all these studies, different processes (e.g., man-
ufacturing and remanufacturing) have independent resources, each of
which has its own capacity.
In our contribution, we compare models with finite and infinite
capacities and analyze the impact of line sharing on the decisions.
With respect to setup costs and time, there are no studies on joint
setups between manufacturing and remanufacturing/refurbishing even
in the case of shared capacity presented by Bayındır et al. (2005) in
their single period model. Further, we analyzed models with joint and
separate setup times and costs. Besides the fact that this is different
from the initial idea of the company (having two completely indepen-
dent lines), sharing the same production line for manufacturing and
remanufacturing has shown its advantages in several situations (Tang
and Teunter, 2006).
All studies on dynamic lot sizing models in Table 1 allow downward
substitution, where the shortage of refurbished products is compen-
sated by the higher value new product, but at the same price as the used
one. Two inventory control models were proposed with bidirectional
substitution. A particular case (also in a continuous time model) is
that of Afshar-Bakeshloo et al. (2021) where the product (tires in this
case) can have three different forms: new, remanufactured once, or
remanufactured twice. The products are destined to prime, budget,
and low budget market segments. The authors propose the successive
downward substitution of products. Afshar-Bakeshloo et al. (2021) use
Markov chains to the model the system. In the case of our study, we
propose different levels of quality and allow downgrading and upgrading
of returned products. In the refurbishing process, a premium returned
electronic product, for example, can be downgraded by using lower
quality components into it.
In terms of objectives, the references in Table 1 can be classified
as profit maximization (e.g., Ahiska et al., 2017; Bayındır et al., 2005)
or cost minimization (e.g., Li et al., 2006; Piñeyro and Viera, 2009).
The results from (Li et al., 2006) show that the cost savings due to
downward substitution can reach 15%. While continuous time models
mostly use Markovian decision processes, dynamic lot sizing model use
metaheuristics such as genetic algorithms (Li et al., 2007), tabu search
(Piñeyro and Viera, 2010) and ant colony optimization (Liu et al.,
2019). In our case, we have proposed an efficient relax-and-fix and
fix-and-optimize heuristic.
The choice of the relax-and-fix and fix-and-optimize heuristic is
motivated by its successful use in solving several lot sizing prob-
lems. Pochet and Wolsey (2006) presents a general scheme of a relax-
and-fix heuristic applied to a generic mixed integer program. For lot
sizing problems, relax-and-fix heuristics have been used by Absi and
van den Heuvel (2019) for the uncapacitated single-item lot sizing
problem, by Federgruen et al. (2007) for the capacitated single level
multi-item lot sizing problem and by Stadtler (2003) and Akartunalı
and Miller (2009) for a multi-level lot sizing problem. Fix-and-optimize
heuristics have been applied by Sahling et al. (2009) and Helber
and Sahling (2010) to a multi-level capacitated lot sizing problem. A
combined approach based on fix-and-relax and fix-and-optimize was
applied by Stadtler and Sahling (2013) to a lot sizing and scheduling
model for multi-stage flow lines with zero lead times and by Toledo
et al. (2015) to multi-level lot sizing problems. Further details regarding
the heuristics are presented in Section 4.

2.3. Summary of contributions
Based on the above literature review and on Table 1, we can
summarize the contributions and particular aspects of our work as
follows. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that a
hybrid system combining refurbishing, manufacturing, and disassembly
processes is modeled and solved. We have justified the usefulness of
such a model in practice through examples from industry.
Our paper is also the first to analyze a model with shared se-
tups between the processing lines. Like several other studies, we have
adopted downward substitution and we have shown the relevance of
this choice in the previous paragraphs. This first deterministic model is
followed by models where the amount of returns and refurbished item’s
demand are input stochastic parameters. A two-stage and risk averse
two-stage models are presented and solved. Finally, a multi-item model
with different quality and finishing levels is considered. The amount of
returns is an input parameter and it is correlated with the demand for
new items of the same grade. Downgrading and upgrading are allowed
at different costs. A detailed discussion of flows between processes is
provided in the numerical experiments section. To solve hard instances
of the problems, an efficient mathematical programming-based heuris-
tic is proposed. The heuristic can obtain solutions close to optimum in
less than 5 s for reasonable size instances.
3. Problem description
Here we describe the problem illustrated in Fig. 1. In this hybrid
system, the production facility receives returned products. One unit of
the returned item is composed of one core part (component) and several
non-core parts (see Liu et al., 2019). The core part can be regarded
as good as new (by undergoing some processes). While the non-core
parts cannot be regarded as good as new. To produce one unit of a
refurbished item (Path 1), we use one unit of a returned item (core
part and non-core parts). But if we want to use a returned item in
manufacturing of new item (Path 2), we disassemble the non-core parts
(waste recovery) from the returned item and keep the one core part to
be used in the assembly of the new item. Some of the returns will be
discarded and sent directly to an independent recycling process (not
shown here). The accepted items are eventually stored in inventory
and separated into two sets. One set of items is refurbished (Path 1
on Fig. 1) and used to satisfy the demand for second-hand products.
The second set of returned items are disassembled to extract the core
part (key component) that can be regarded as good as new as indicated
previously. Most prior studies implicitly assume that all returns are
of the same condition, i.e. homogeneous quality, and thus require the
same processing and holding costs. For example, from each unit of TVs
returned, the part or subassemblies recovered may be: control board ,
power cables, mother board, etc. The key parts extracted from each unit
of item returned are approximately the same. The group of components
recovered from one unit of a returned item is called a part in the rest
of paper. Each part is used in the manufacturing of one unit of the
new product (Path 2). The latter can also be manufactured using the
new purchased parts (Path 3). The remaining components and parts
resulting from the disassembly can enter the recycling process. Given
certain conditions, the company is also ready to substitute secondhand
products with new ones at the same price, if this is economically feasi-
ble. Fig. 2 illustrates the one-to-one relationship between refurbished,
disassembled, and manufactured products and parts. The upper part of
the figure shows that for one unit of the returned items, the process

upgrading operations. The lower part shows that from one unit of the
returned and disassembled item, we extract one unit of the ‘‘key’’ part

Continuing with the description of the problem in Fig. 1, Path 3 in-
dicates the case where the core part of the product is purchased instead
of being recovered from a collected item. Finally, Path 4 represents the
case where the refurbishing task is done on the same production line of
the new items, requiring a changeover time between refurbishing and
assembly of new items on this line.
The objective is to maximize the total profit calculated as the
difference between the revenue generated from the sales of items
(new and refurbished) and the total cost which includes disassembly,
manufacturing, stock-out penalty, and inventory holding costs.
The demand for new items must be fully satisfied. However, stock-
out is allowed for the refurbished items. When this happens, such
demand can be either satisfied through downward substitution from
new items, or is considered as a lost sale, which translates into a penalty
cost in the objective function. Demand is time dependent over a discrete
time horizon of 𝑇 time periods. For each time period of the planning
horizon (𝑡 = 1...𝑇 ), the planner must decide on:
• the quantity to be disassembled and used for manufacturing of
new products,
• the quantity of new parts to be purchased,
• the quantity of new products to be manufactured,
• the quantity of used products that will be refurbished or disas-
sembled, and
• the quantity of new products that will be used for substitution
The quantity of products that can be collected and stored to enter
the process is considered as decision variable in Section 3.1 and as input
parameter in Sections 3.2.2 and 3.3.
Assumptions
The following assumptions are made to develop the model:
• All the accepted returns can be used for refurbishing or manufac-
turing.
• Both market-driven and waste stream systems (Guide, 2001) are
used to represent how used items are obtained.
– The market-driven approach relies on end-users to return
their products to a firm specializing in the reuse of those
products. End-users are motivated to return end-of-life prod-
ucts by financial incentives, such as deposit systems, credit
toward a new unit, or cash paid for a specified level of
quality. The level of returns is a decision variable in this
case as suggested in the model of Section 3.1.
– The waste stream approach relies on diverting discarded
products from landfilling by making producers responsible
for the collection and reuse of their products. In this situ-
ation, firms passively accept all product returns from the
waste stream and the level of returns is a parameter as
suggested in Sections 3.2 and 3.3.
• There are two alternatives (process flexibility) for refurbishing
with a different costs. The first is to do the refurbishing in the
specialized workshop with high setup but a reduced unit cost.
The second is to integrate refurbishing with manufacturing with
reduced setup cost but high unit cost and major (minor) setup
times.

1. A single grade (single quality level) model with shared or sepa-
rate resources/capacities between refurbishing and manufactur-
ing of new products and shared or separate inventory of returns
for refurbishing and disassembly (Section 3.1).
2. A single grade model with uncertainty on returns and used
product demands.
3. A multi grade (multi quality levels) model.

3.2. Single grade stochastic problem
The following model attempts to represent the situation with un-
certainty on some demands (here 𝑑𝑟
𝑡 ) and the amount of returns. This
model corresponds to the waste stream approach for obtaining used
items. In this situation, firms passively accept all product returns from
the waste stream and the level of returns is a parameter. We still assume
a single type of products as in Section 3.1. However, the returns amount
is an input parameter instead of being a decision variable. There exist
different approaches to tackle optimization problems with uncertainty
on parameters. These include, for example, robust optimization and
stochastic programming. The latter has received a lot of importance in
solving many different optimization problems with uncertainty. Com-
pared to robust optimization, stochastic programming assumes that the
uncertain parameters follow a known probability distribution. For an
introduction to stochastic programming and its applications, we refer
to the book of Birge and Louveaux (2011).
In stochastic programming, the two-stage formulation has been
widely used in the literature. Its basic idea is that decisions should
be based on data available at the time the decisions are made. Such
decisions should not depend on future observations. Hence, decisions
are made in two stages and the observation of some random event takes
place in between. The first set of decisions, that must be taken before
the observation of any of the uncertain elements in the problem, are
called first-stage decisions. The second set of decisions, that can be
taken after the occurrence of uncertain events, are called second stage
decisions.
Return and demand uncertainties are the common uncertain param-
eters in the literature of production planning with returns (Ferguson
et al., 2009; Azizi et al., 2020). As problems with uncertain parameters
represented with continuous distribution are computationally chal-
lenging, scenarios are typically used to approximate the underlying
continuous distributions. Each scenario is a set of discrete values show-
ing returns from the primary markets and demands in the secondary
markets (Azizi et al., 2020).
In this section, a two-stage stochastic programming is proposed
where demand for refurbished products 𝑑𝑟 and the amount of returned
products 𝑞𝑐 are random parameters. In this case, the first-stage variables
are used to define the quantities to be manufactured, refurbished,
disassembled, and purchased (𝑞𝑎, 𝑞𝑟, 𝑞𝑑, 𝑝𝑛) and their respective setups
(𝑦𝑎, 𝑦𝑟, 𝑦𝑑, 𝑦𝑛). The second stage refers to the inventory levels ( 𝐼 𝑎, 𝐼 𝑟,
𝐼 𝑑, 𝐼 𝑛, 𝐼 𝑐), the quantities used for satisfying demand for refurbished
products (𝑞𝑠, 𝑞𝑟𝑑) and the stock-out level 𝐿𝑟.
However, the quality of the uncertain information may not be
good enough to be expressed as probability distributions; even if the
probability distributions are available, it could be difficult to reflect
them in large scale models. Consequently, interval parameters are
introduced into the stochastic programming framework to facilitate
communication of the uncertainties into the optimization process, that
can be reasonably well described by a finite set of realizations called
scenarios. For each scenario, there is a corresponding probability of
occurrence.

3.2.2. Risk-averse two-stage stochastic model
The main idea of a risk averse stochastic models is to reduce the
variability of specific outcome realizations over the scenarios by intro-
ducing a variable that measures the robustness of the model. Common
risk measures used include risk deviation that evaluate the dispersion
of the second part related to the second stage variables in the objective
function.
Among the different risk deviation measures, we adopt the well-
known upper partial mean that is defined as the expected value of the
positive deviation (Mulvey et al., 1995), and we introduce a new deci-
sion variable 𝛿𝜉 to evaluate the deviation between the total profit of the
scenario 𝜉 and the expected total profit. This variable is inserted into
constraints and penalized into the objective function by a parameter
𝜆 > 0. A similar approach was proposed in Macedo et al. (2016) on a
lot sizing problem with remanufacturing.

3.3. Multi grade deterministic problem formulation
Vertical differentiation is a strategy that has been adopted by com-
panies for a long time. Vertical differentiation is related to offering
products with different quality levels but offering essentially the same
functionalities. The products (models) can be classified in this case as
high, medium, and low-quality; or equivalently as premium, standard
and basic models (Randall et al., 1998). An example, is the Samsung
Galaxy Note 10 Plus, Note 10, and Note 10 Lite. The three models offer
basically the same functionalities but differ in some performance and
finishing levels. Another example is the Nikon SLR 35 mm cameras
N50, N70, and N90 models, which again differ only in quality level
(Randall et al., 1998). Such differentiation is made to satisfy customers’
different needs at different price levels.
The quality level or grade should be taken considered when re-
turned items are received. A priori, a product of a given quality level
should be refurbished at the same level of quality. For example, a
premium returned product should be refurbished to satisfy the demand
for a premium used product. It is important to highlight the difference
between the quality grading concept related to vertical differentiation
as defined above from the grading of returns as an operational decision
where returned items are tested for any technical issues are categorized
(graded) based on the required efforts and costs to refurbish or reman-
ufacture them (Ferguson et al., 2009). Though such grading systems
have proven their importance to increase profitability as indicated in
the paper of Ferguson et al. (2009), this option is not considered in
the current work as our focus is on the analysis of upgrading and
downgrading of different product models (grades).
Upgrading and downgrading occur mainly in refurbishing. Upgrad-
ing a low-quality product (a basic model, for example) consists of
replacing worn-out parts of such model with parts extracted from a
returned product of high quality (a premium model, for example).
Downgrading occurs in the opposite direction where parts from a
returned low-quality product are used in a high quality product. These
operations are possible because the vertical differentiation usually al-
lows some level of compatibility between spare parts of the products.
For example, an S-pen can be used in the Samsung Galaxy Note 10 Lite,
10, and 10 Plus models, though the S-pen of the Galaxy Note 10 Plus
might have underdone higher quality inspection efforts compared to
the one bundled with the Galaxy Note 10 Lite.

4. A heuristic solution approach
It should be noted that this problem is NP-hard. This is a generaliza-
tion of the One Warehouse Multi Retailer (OWMR) problem, which was
proven to be NP-hard by Arkin et al. (1989). For this reason, heuristic
techniques are adopted to solve it. With the growing performance of
MILP-solvers, MILP-based heuristics have been employed to solve MILP
models. Among the existing MILP-based heuristics, we have chosen
relax-and-fix heuristic and fix-and-optimize heuristic.
The main idea of relax-and-fix heuristics (RF) consists of decom-
posing a MILP model to iteratively solve sub-problems with a reduced
number of variables with integrality constraints. In the case of a MILP
with binary variables, the latter are partitioned into three subsets
in each iteration of the procedure. The first subset is set to fixed
binary values (from previous iterations). The second subset is relaxed
to take arbitrary continuous values between 0 and 1. The third subset
is restricted with integrality constraints to take only values 0 or 1.
This reduces the number of binary variables in the sub-problem of
the current iteration. The solution time for a sub-problem is very
small. Relax-and-fix heuristics were developed for different production
planning and lot sizing problems. More details of the relax-and-fix
heuristic for lot sizing problems can be found in Pochet and Wolsey
(1991).
Fix-and-optimize heuristic (FO) optimizes a subset of variables by
solving a series of sub-problems in an iterative manner. A sub-problem
is the reduced form of the original problem where a set of decision
variables are fixed to integer values (sometimes randomly) whereas the
remaining set are left to be optimized. Like the relax-and-fix heuristics,
the FO heuristics are known for their ease of implementation, and thus,
have been commonly adopted in various lot sizing problems over the
last decade. For more information on the fix-and-optimize heuristic, the
reader can refer to the studies of Pochet and Wolsey (1991) and Sahling
et al. (2009).
The next sections present the two phases of a Time-based relax-and-
fix and fix-and-optimize heuristic (TRF-FO). In the first phase, a good
feasible solution using time-based relax-and-fix construction heuristic
(TRF) presented in Section 4.1. In the second phase, an improvement
process is executed on this solution using fix-and-optimize heuristic
(FO) as shown in Section 4.2.
4.1. Time-based relax-and-fix heuristic (TRF)
The time-based relax-and-fix heuristic is based on the decomposi-
tion of the planning horizon into three intervals or windows of periods,
which represent internally rolled horizons. In the first window of
periods, which we call 𝑊 𝐹 , the binary variables are fixed to their
optimal value from the solution of a MILP sub-problem solved in the
previous iterations. In the second window of periods, called 𝑊 𝐼, the
setup variables are forced to be binary. The binary variables in the last
window, 𝑊 𝑅, are relaxed and take any real value between 0 and 1. The
length of each window is determined using to two parameters, 𝛼 and
𝛽, where 𝛼 and 𝛽 are integer values and 𝛽 < 𝛼 . Then, iteratively, the
windows will be rolling from the beginning of the planning horizon
to the end as illustrated in Fig. 3. In the first iteration, a MILP sub-

4.2. Fix-and-optimize heuristic (FO)
The FO heuristic (summarized in Algorithm 2) starts from an initial
solution and considers all integrality constraints at every iteration
(i.e. no binary variables are relaxed). It is based on the decomposition
of the binary variables into two subsets. The first subset is made of
variables whose values are fixed to 0 or 1. The second subset is com-
posed of free binary variables (with integrality constraints). There are
different ways to choose the fixed and the to-be-optimized variables.
The most well known approach is a time-based approach where the
planning horizon is divided into two windows of periods, 𝑊 𝐹 and
𝑊 𝐼, which are internally rolled horizons. 𝑊 𝐼 is the subset of periods
where the setup variables are to be re-optimized. The length of 𝑊 𝐼
window is equal to parameter 𝛾 which can take its values arbitrarily
between [𝛾𝑚𝑖𝑛, 𝛾𝑚𝑎𝑥]. The 𝑊 𝐹 subset includes the remaining periods
where the binary variables are fixed to their optimal value from the
solution of an MILP sub-problem solved in the previous iterations. An
illustration of the sliding of window 𝑊 𝐼 is shown on Fig. 4. Starting
with an initial solution 𝑆0 which can be obtained by a construction
heuristic (TRF heuristic in case of TRF-FO approach), the first iteration
solves a MILP sub-problem where binary variables in the first 𝛾 periods
are reoptimized while the remaining variables are fixed. In the next
iteration, if there is no improvement in the objective function compared
to the best solution so far, then we slide the 𝑊 𝐼 window by 𝛾 periods,
update 𝑊 𝐹 and solve a MILP sub-problem. If there is an improvement
in the objective function, the best solution is updated as the current
solution. Then we restart 𝑊 𝐼 window from the beginning of the hori-
zon with a new value of 𝛾 (chosen randomly between [𝛾 𝑚𝑖𝑛, 𝛾 𝑚𝑎𝑥]) and
we reset iteration counter to zero. The FO heuristic stops when there is
no improvement and 𝑊 𝐼 window reaches the end of the horizon. The
MILP sub-problem solved in FO heuristic (we call it 𝐹 𝑂𝐿𝑆) is the same
as 𝑇 𝑅𝐹 𝐿𝑆 without constraints (58).

5. Numerical experiments
The numerical experiments are divided into two sections: the first
section focuses on computational performance of the exact (solver)
approach and the heuristic procedure. The second section provides
managerial insights.
The computational experiments analyze the complexity of the single
grade deterministic problem (SGDM) described in Section 3 under
different as a function of different problem parameters. The experiment
have two main objectives:
• For small size instances, the numerical performance of the pro-
posed heuristic is evaluated by comparing the best solution with
the optimal solution obtained by a commercial MILP solver.
• For larger size instances, where the MILP solver was not able to
find any optimal solutions in reasonable CPU time, a limited time
is fixed to the solver and the results obtained are compared with
those obtained by heuristic approach.
The managerial insight experiments study the environmental and
economic impact of the key parameters. The objective of this experi-
ments is threefold:
• For the single grade deterministic model (SGDM), the impacts
of variations in unit costs on different indicators were analyzed.
The indicators include profit, collection efficiency, substitution,
and lost sales. This analysis allows decision makers interested in
hybrid refurbishing and manufacturing systems to identify condi-
tions and parameters that impact such performance indicators.

5.1. Data generation
The key parameters such as the length of the planning horizon 𝑇 ,
the demand of new products 𝑑𝑎, the setup costs for manufacturing
𝐶 𝑠𝑎, and the holding cost for new manufactured products ℎ𝑎 are gen-
erated from Teunter et al. (2006), among others. The other parameters
are generated according to the key parameters, within a reasonable
range, and to make sure that all processes (collection, disassembly,
refurbishing, refurbishing in shared line, purchasing) of new parts,
manufacturing and substitution take place.
The description of the default values (nominal value) of all param-
eters is discussed as follows:
• The length of the planning horizon is 𝑇 = 12.
• Manufacturing and refurbishing capacities, 𝑄𝑎 and 𝑄𝑟 are fixed
to 200 and 100, respectively.
• Demands 𝑑𝑎 and 𝑑𝑟 are a ratio of capacities and have been
generated randomly from a normal distribution with a mean 𝜇𝑎,
𝜇𝑟 and a standard deviations 𝜎𝑎, 𝜎𝑟 respectively. 𝜇𝑎 = 0 .5 × 𝑄𝑎,

purchasing) are a percentage of manufacturing cost as follows:

6. Conclusions and future work
We studied the production planning problem in a facility that
receives returned products. We have proposed a profit maximization
model for dynamic lot sizing considering substitution and multiple
usage of returns for refurbishing at different levels of quality or for
disassembly to extract key parts that will be used in the manufacturing
process. Discussion started with a single grade (single level of quality)
and was extended to multi grade case. This model allowed to study
interactions between different types of returns and decisions. The im-
pact of uncertainty on the different interactions was analyzed through
a robust two-stage stochastic program with uncertainty on demands
and returns. The models were implemented as mixed integer linear
programming formulations. These optimization problems are NP-hard.
Therefore, we proposed an efficient heuristic based on relax-and-fix and
fix-and-optimize approaches. The heuristic give solutions very close to
optimum in few seconds on problems with reasonable lengths of the
planning horizon.
Several managerial insights were derived through extensive numeri-
cal experiments. The main findings are as follows. From an economical
point of view, the profit margin of the refurbished items is the main
determinant of the total profit and when such margin in high, the
total profit becomes more sensitive to different cost variations. With
respect to the collection efficiency (environment-related performance
indicator), it becomes very sensitive to collection cost and much less
sensitive to refurbishing cost. This was more remarkable with high
profit margins on the refurbished items. A third finding was related to
the two-stage stochastic model. As uncertainty level increases, risk of
stock-out increases. The best option chosen by the model to compensate
for these stock-outs is substitution instead of more refurbishing. Based
