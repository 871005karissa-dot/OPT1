ABSTRACT 
The classification of products into grades based on the tolerances of key quality characteris -
tics specified by customers is a common practice aligning with diverse customer needs and 
reducing scrap. This study is mainly to assess system reliability, the probability that the man -
ufacturing system fulfils orders of all grades, as a performance index to determine the man -
ageable order quantity to accept. The manufacturing system is first modelled as a multistate 
flow network (MFN) to analyse material flows across workstations required to satisfy the 
demands of all grades while incorporating the multiple capacities of each workstation 
caused by machine uncertainty. Then, two practical demand fulfilment policies are consid -
ered: satisfying demand for a grade with products of the same grade or through downward 
substitution. Lastly, the determination of the lower capacity vector (LCV) that meets the 
demands of all grades is utilised as an efficient method for calculating system reliability, 
with an algorithm proposed to generate the LCV under two policies. Several experiments 
are conducted to observe the changes in system reliability and profit of varying demands 
under the two policies, enabling managers of manufacturing companies to adjust order vol -
umes and select the policy that best aligns with the objective of the company.
PRACTITIONER SUMMARY 
A production system naturally produces products of different quality due to process variabil -
ity. These products are often divided into different quality grades, each with its own demand. 
This study provides actionable insights to support production managers in making order man -
agement decisions when facing orders of products across these grades. Same-grade fulfilment 
(Policy I) and downward substitution (Policy II) are considered. System reliability, the probabil -
ity that a manufacturing system can produce enough products to meet demand for all grades, 
is adopted as a performance index. By evaluating system reliability and associated profit, we 
observe a trade-off between the two policies: one performs better in profit, while the other 
performs better in system reliability. When profit is the priority, Policy I is preferred, although 
its system reliability is relatively low. For important or urgent orders, where unmet demand 
could hurt customer satisfaction or long-term relationships, Policy II is recommended to 
ensure higher system reliability. Managers should also be aware that prolonged storage under 
Policy I may gradually reduce its profit advantage. These findings help managers adjust order 
volumes and select the fulfilment policy that aligns with their objectives.

1. Introduction
The same manufacturing system, operating under 
identical processes, may simultaneously produce prod -
ucts with varying quality levels (Kalay & Tas ¸kın, 2021 ; 
Lu et al., 2019 ). Manufacturers typically aim to meet 
specified quality requirements, and products failing to 
conform are often discarded. However, customer 
expectations for the same product vary, with some 
accepting more relaxed standards (Gupta & Khanna, 
2024 ; Kazemi et al., 2018 ). Ignoring these variations 
can result in significant losses from scrapped products 
and missed opportunities in lower-quality markets. To 
address this challenge, categorising products into mul -
tiple grades has been widely adopted as an effective 
approach to allocate products according to customer- 
specific quality requirements (Transchel et al., 2016 ). 
For instance, in the semiconductor industry, binning 
enables the sale of chips with slightly lower perform -
ance to customers with lower quality demands, 
thereby reducing scrap rates and enhancing overall 
profitability (Hsieh et al., 2011 ). Moreover, product 
grading creates demands for multiple quality levels, 
making it a critical concern for manufacturers to fulfil 
all grade-specific demands.

When products are classified into multiple grades, 
demand fulfilment becomes more complex than in the 
single-grade case. Higher-quality products, due to 
tighter tolerances, are generally associated with super -
ior functionality and higher market prices (Zhang 
et al., 2019 ). To maintain consistency between product 
value and customer expectations, orders are usually 
fulfilled with products of the same grade (Lu et al., 
2019 ), referred to as same-grade fulfilment (named 
Policy I herein). However, the output of a particular 
grade may sometimes be insufficient to satisfy its 
demand, which can result in customer dissatisfaction 
and penalties. To address this issue, using higher-qual -
ity products to fulfil orders for lower-quality products 
has been adopted in practice (Ahiska et al., 2017 ; Wu 
et al., 2020 ), allowing manufacturers to meet demand 
while mitigating negative consequences, referred to 
downward substitution (named Policy II herein). The 
ability of a manufacturing system to fulfil multi-grade 
demand is directly influenced by these two policies. 
Therefore, their impacts on system performance are 
systematically assessed in this study.
A manufacturing system is a complex network in 
which raw materials and work-in-progress (WIP) are 
sequentially processed across multiple workstations to 
meet the required specifications. Whether the target 
quantity can be achieved depends on the capacity 
available at each workstation. When multiple grade 
orders are placed from diverse customers, order fulfil -
ment capability becomes a critical concern for manag -
ers, since failing to satisfy demand may lead to 
breaches of contract, loss of customer trust, or even 
penalties (Craig et al., 2021 ; Shen et al., 2022 ). To 
assess such capability, many studies (Chang et al., 
2024 ; Niu et al., 2023 ; Su et al., 2024 ; Yeh et al., 2023 ) 
have adopted system reliability as an effective metric, 
as it intuitively reflects the probability of successfully 
achieving the intended function of a system, while also 
allowing other considerations such as cost and time to 
be incorporated as constraints to address various man -
agerial concerns. In this study, system reliability is 
defined as the probability that the manufacturing sys -
tem can fulfil demand across all product grades, serv -
ing as a decision-support metric for managers in 
order acceptance.
In manufacturing systems, parallel configurations 
are commonplace, where each workstation is config -
ured with multiple identical machines arranged in 
parallel (Ahmadi et al., 2024 ; Lin et al., 2016 ). Even 
if some machines are under maintenance, the work -
station can continue to operate based on the num -
ber of machines in service, reflecting its multiple 
capacities. Therefore, the manufacturing system is 
modelled as a multistate flow network (MFN) to 
assess system reliability in many studies (Chang 
et al., 2017 ; Lin & Chang, 2012 ; Yang et al., 2024 ; 
Yao et al., 2024 ). By transforming the system into a 
network topology composed of nodes and edges, 
network analysis and the concept of minimal paths 
can be applied to analyse material flows that fulfil 
demand (Niu et al., 2020 ; Yeh, 2018 ). The concept 
of lower capacity vector (LCV), an efficient tech -
nique to calculate system reliability, is widely 
applied in related studies (Chang et al., 2024 ; Huang 
& Lin, 2024 ; Lin et al., 1995 ; Lin & Chang, 2012 ).
Although various MFN models have been devel -
oped to evaluate system reliability, existing studies 
primarily focus on single-product demands and 
have not addressed multi-grade demand and related 
fulfilment policies. Moreover, research on product 
grading and downward substitution has not consid -
ered the multistate nature of manufacturing systems. 
To address this gap, the aim of this study is to 
evaluate the system reliability of a manufacturing 
system under two demand fulfilment policies: same- 
grade fulfilment and downward substitution. When 
applying product grading, higher-quality grades nat -
urally have higher value (market price), and demand 
is typically fulfilled using the same-grade products. 
However, due to variability in the manufacturing 
system, each grade is produced in a different pro -
portion of the total output. Fulfilling demand using 
the same-grade products can result in either the 
overproduction of higher-quality grades or lower- 
quality grades, or in a shortfall of products for a 
specific grade. Compared with same-grade fulfil -
ment, downward substitution is a widely adopted 
alternative in practice, provides greater flexibility in 
resource utilisation. When excess higher-grade prod -
ucts are available, they can be used to satisfy lower- 
grade demand. Thus, overproduction of a specific 
grade is less likely to occur under downward substi -
tution; however, revenue loss will be incurred due 
to high-value products being sold at lower prices. 
Since the probability of successful order fulfilment 
and profit are regarded as major considerations by 
manufacturing managers, these two demand fulfil -
ment policies are taken into account in this study. 
An algorithm is developed to generate the lower 
capacity vector (LCV) for either policy, enabling 
efficient assessment of system reliability. Profit ana -
lysis is further conducted for both policies to 
observe the influence on system reliability. By inte -
grating system reliability and profit analysis, man -
agerial insights are provided to assist in the 
selection of appropriate fulfilment policies and the 
adjustment of order quantities for effective order 
acceptance decisions.

2. Literature review
The relevant literature for this study is focused on 
product grading, downward substitution, and system 
reliability assessment. The relevance of this study to 
these research areas is discussed below.
2.1. Product grading
Products are typically classified through full inspec -
tion based on multiple acceptable standards or toler -
ances for key quality characteristics (KQCs) or 
attributes to match them to their corresponding 
applications (Kolus & Duffuaa, 2025 ). Several stud -
ies (Kolus & Duffuaa, 2025 ; Lahmar et al., 2025 ; 
Park et al., 2021 ; Raza & Turiac, 2016 ; Tang, 1990 ; 
Zhang et al., 2019 ) have developed models to assess 
product grades and evaluate their impact on profit. 
Zhang et al. ( 2019 ) proposed a piecewise quadratic 
loss function for hierarchical products to estimate 
expected losses across different grades and select the 
most economical material. Lahmar et al. ( 2025 ) clas -
sified remanufactured products into different quality 
grades, each with distinct manufacturing processes 
and CO
2 
emission coefficients, and applied the non- 
dominated genetic sorting algorithm (NSGA-II), 
entropy, and technique for order preference by simi -
larity to ideal solution (TOPSIS) to prioritise high- 
quality order demand while minimising cost and 
emissions. The improvement of product grading 
efficiency has also attracted attention in several 
studies (Hsieh et al., 2011 ; Jia et al., 2021 ; Kim & 
Lee, 2023 ). Hsieh et al. ( 2011 ) proposed an adaptive 
error-tolerance-based testing method to enhance 
yield by grading chips according to error rates 
while minimising test costs. Jia et al. ( 2021 ) used 
27-dimensional RGB colour-difference vectors as 
input and applied partial least squares discriminant 
analysis to build a “small-sample, high-dimensional” 
model that reduces dimensionality while enhancing 
classification performance, enabling quick identifica -
tion of tea product categories and grades. Kim 
and Lee ( 2023 ) developed a neural-network-based 
approach using in-mold sensors and vibration data 
to predict the quality grade of each moulded part in 
real time, enabling timely reduction of defects and 
waste. While these studies investigate how to more 
efficiently classify products, improve grading effi -
ciency, and achieve higher profitability, they either 
do not address demand satisfaction or assume fixed 
capacity values, without considering the multistate 
nature of capacity in manufacturing systems.
2.2. Downward substitution
Downward substitution is commonly applied in 
manufacturing and inventory management when 
orders for lower-quality products cannot be fulfilled 
and are instead satisfied using available higher-qual -
ity or premium products (Ahiska et al., 2017 ; 
Freeman et al., 2021 ; Luo & Chen, 2022 ; Transchel 
et al., 2016 ; Wu et al., 2020 ; Yu et al., 2017 ; Zheng & 
Gershwin, 2015 ). Downward substitution between 
products produced on distinct production lines has 
been the focus of some studies (Ahiska et al., 2017 ; 
Notz & Pibernik, 2022 ; Yu et al., 2017 ) supporting 
production planning. Notz and Pibernik ( 2022 ) 
developed a kERM-based two-stage capacity plan -
ning model with fixed capacities for multiple produc -
tion lines, each producing a specific product grade; 
downward substitution is incorporated, allowing 
higher-level production lines to satisfy demands 
requiring lower expertise. Yu et al. ( 2017 ) used a 
Markov decision process to determine the optimal 
joint policy for a manufacturing system with pre -
mium and standard production lines, considering 
production and substitution decisions alongside 
dynamic pricing strategies. Some studies (Luo & 
Chen, 2022 ; Wu et al., 2020 ) have investigated sourc -
ing products of different quality directly from suppli -
ers to determine optimal ordering policies. Wu et al. 
( 2020 ) developed a mathematical model that incor -
porates product substitution and dual sourcing strat -
egies under partial production line disruptions, using 
a greedy allocation algorithm to determine optimal 
allocation and minimise expected costs. Luo and 
Chen ( 2022 ) investigated a single-period hospital 
platelet inventory problem, considering heteroge -
neous age-based demand, random supply of young 
platelets, and the effect of unidirectional substitution. 
They determined the optimal ordering policies using 
dynamic programming and analysed their impact on 
service level and expected cost. Overall, these studies 
have primarily addressed issues related to maximis -
ing profit in production planning and purchasing 
from the perspective of inventory management. In 
addition, studies related to manufacturing system 
capacity either ignore uncertainty and treat capacity 
as a fixed value, or consider only the capacity of an 
entire production line in binary states, without 
accounting for multiple possible capacities. In this 
study, downward substitution is treated as an

2.3. System reliability assessment
Over the past decades, the system reliability assess -
ment has received increasing attention, as it offers a 
comprehensive overview of the performance of real- 
world systems. Because such systems often exhibit 
complex configurations and are concerned with ful -
filling demand rather than simply maintaining oper -
ation, network analysis is commonly employed in 
system reliability assessment models to facilitate the 
effective representation and analysis of material or 
information flows (Lin et al., 2016 ; Wang et al., 
2022 ). For system reliability assessment in manufac -
turing systems, many studies (Chang, 2019 ; Huang 
& Lin, 2024 ; Liao et al., 2023 ; Lin & Chang, 2012 ; 
Yang et al., 2024 ; Yao et al., 2024 ) model the prac -
tical manufacturing system as a MFN based on dif -
ferent production scenarios, with multiple capacities 
and their associated probabilities used to evaluate 
system reliability. Yao et al. ( 2024 ) proposed an 
extended MFN that integrates a Markov process to 
capture degradation driven by heterogeneous feed -
stock and thereby more accurately assess system 
reliability. Huang and Lin ( 2024 ) further developed 
a long short-term memory recurrent neural network 
model designed to enhance the efficiency of system 
reliability prediction and provide a basis for main -
tenance decision-making. As many studies assume 
buffer to be infinite, to address finite buffers which 
cause material flow blockage and starvation between 
workstations and buffers, Chang ( 2019 ) incorpo -
rated a Monte Carlo simulation to estimate the sys -
tem reliability. However, only the ability to fulfil a 
single demand is considered in these studies, while 
scenarios involving multiple product grades have 
not been addressed. To bridge this gap, system reli -
ability in fulfilling orders across multiple product 
grades is assessed using the proposed method, with 
the two different demand fulfilment policies consid -
ered specifically in the context of product grading.
3. Model formulation and system reliability 
assessment
In this section, the problem description and 
assumptions of this study are first outlined and dis -
cussed. Next, the approach of product grading is 
presented. Then, the formulation of a manufacturing 
system into an MFN is introduced. Lastly, the sys -
tem reliability assessment method is presented.
3.1. Problem descriptions and assumptions
Different from the references (Ahiska et al., 2017 ; 
Notz & Pibernik, 2022 ; Yu et al., 2017 ), this study 
considers that products of different grades are pro -
duced on the same production line. Product grading 
differentiates products according to heterogeneous 
customer requirements. For example, in smartphone 
camera lens module production, modules are classi -
fied into two grades based on three KQCs: modula -
tion transfer functions (MTFs) at the centre, middle, 
and edge field. MTF reflects lens resolution and is 
expressed as a percentage: 80%–100% for excellent 
grade, 50%–80% for acceptable grade. A module is 
classified as an excellent grade only if all field MTFs 
are 80%–100%; if any field MTF is 50%–80% and 
the others are 80%–100%, it is an acceptable grade; 
otherwise, it is defective. Excellent-grade modules 
are primarily intended for top-tier devices, whereas 
acceptable-grade modules are used for lower-tier 
devices. Customers demand both grades, with excel -
lent-grade products featuring tighter tolerances and 
higher market value. Therefore, the problem 
addressed in this study is assessing system reliability 
for fulfilling multi-grade orders; in other words, it 
examines the extent that a manufacturing system 
has sufficient capacity to meet multi-grade demand 
under machine uncertainties and limited resources. 
We consider a manufacturing system that consists 
of multiple workstations arranged in series, where 
raw materials or WIPs must visit all workstations 
sequentially to meet the specified tolerances of all 
KQCs. Typically, each workstation is composed of 
one or multiple identical machines arranged in a 
parallel configuration. To facilitate analysis of 
material flow, the manufacturing system is modelled 
as a network topology, with workstations repre -
sented as edges and inspections/buffer storage repre -
sented as nodes. Then, the system reliability of the 
manufacturing system is assessed for multi-grade 
order acceptance decision. The problem is studied 
under the following assumptions:
1. The capacities of edges are statistically inde -
pendent (Lin et al., 2016 ; Yang et al., 2022 ).
2. Non-destructive quality inspections, movement 
of WIP, and buffer storage between worksta -
tions will not cause conforming items to 
become defective.
3. The specifications of KQCs are statistically inde -
pendent. (He et al., 2017 ; Wang et al., 2022 ; 
Yang et al., 2024 ).

3.2. Product grading
The produced products are classified into grades 
based on KQCs. Generally, quality characteristics 
can be classified into three types: nominal-the-best 
(NTB), smaller-the-best (STB), and larger-the-best 
(LTB) characteristics. A product is accepted only if 
all specifications meet the defined tolerances, which

a target value together with either the LSL or the 
USL. Specifications outside these tolerances are con -
sidered defective. Some defective products may still 
be sold at a discount or accepted by other custom -
ers, depending on quality and price. Accordingly, 
products can be classified into multiple grades based 
on these tolerances. The simplest case of categoris -
ing products into grades is based on the tolerances 
of a single KQC. For grade g, the LSL and USL are

6.2. Managerial implications
There is a clear trade-off between system reliability 
and profit for Policy I and Policy II. Policy I is asso -
ciated with higher profit but lower system reliability, 
whereas Policy II is associated with higher system 
reliability but lower profit. Based on the results for 
system reliability and profit, actionable insights for 
managers are summarised as follows:
1. Under a profit-oriented managerial objective, 
Policy I is a preferable option for managers. 
However, the main drawback of Policy I lies in 
its relatively low system reliability. To avoid an 
excessively low system reliability, a feasible 
approach is to reduce the demand for a specific 
grade. This reduction can be implemented at 
the order acceptance stage, where the manufac -
turer determines the maximum order quantity 
that can be accepted for each grade to achieve 
an acceptable level of system reliability, 
and then communicates the available supply 
quantity to customers. For example, if the

7. Conclusion
This study is mainly to assess the system reliability 
and profit of a manufacturing system in the context 
of fulfilling demands for products of different grades 
under the same-grade fulfilment (Policy I) and 
downward substitution (Policy II). System reliability 
refers to the probability that the manufacturing sys -
tem can meet the demands of all grades, serving as 
an intuitive performance index for order acceptance 
decisions. To evaluate system reliability, the manu -
facturing system is modelled as a multistate flow 
network (MFN). Next, we derive the lower capacity 
vector (LCV) based on the relationship between the 
workload and the multiple capacities of each work -
station, enabling efficient calculation of system reli -
ability. Experimental results reveal a clear trade-off 
between system reliability and profit. Policy II 
improves system reliability and reduces inventory, 
but results in lower profit. Conversely, Policy I 
achieves higher profit despite lower system reliabil -
ity; nevertheless, prolonged storage duration may 
gradually offset its profit advantage.
The main contributions of this study are three -
fold. First, the gap in current system reliability 
assessment studies is bridged, as production grading 
and demand fulfilment policies have not been 
addressed, and studies on product grading and 
downward substitution have overlooked the multi -
state nature of manufacturing systems. Therefore, a 
novel MFN model is established to consider the ful -
filment of multi-grade orders. Second, an algorithm 
is developed to generate LCV for computing system 
reliability under Policy I (same-grade fulfilment) 
and Policy II (downward substitution), with a proof 
provided for deriving the amount of input required 
to fulfil demands under Policy II. Finally, the practi -
cality of the proposed algorithm is demonstrated 
through a case study of a ball screw pair manufac -
turing system. By jointly considering system reliabil -
ity and profit, managers can identify the most 
appropriate policy. When profit is prioritised, Policy 
I is preferred, whereas Policy II is adopted to ensure 
higher system reliability and secure order fulfilment.
